When is a model in doubt?

Two kinds of doubt, a way to measure one of them, and what the entropy of a single token does and does not tell you.

8 Oct 2026 · 4 min

A language model states every answer in the same even tone. Nothing in the text says how sure it was. Our task: take one answer and score how much the model doubts it, as the probability the model itself puts on that answer being true.

This is not about right and wrong. A model taught a wrong fact can hold it with full confidence, and that is fine here; correctness is a separate question. We want the doubt, and only the doubt. That needs a definition first, because "doubt" covers more than one thing.

Two kinds of doubt

A hunch that something is off. The answer does not sit well with everything else the model knows. Its general knowledge points the other way: the truth is not that, or something different. The answer breaks the logic the model has learnt, and the conflict is the doubt.

Many roads, many answers. There is more than one way to reach an answer, and different ways land in different places. Here doubt is spread: ask again, or reason differently, and the answer moves.

The first is a question of coherence with what the model knows. The second is a question of variance in what it says. This post is about the second.

Doubt as variance

Variance is easy to compute for a number or a vector. An answer is neither. It is a phrase, often several tokens long, and two phrases can differ in every token and still mean the same thing: Paris, the city of Paris, It's Paris. What matters is semantic variance: how far apart the answers are in meaning, not in wording.

A probabilistic model helps here. Every answer it can give comes with a probability, its stake on that answer being true. The spread of a probability distribution has a standard measure, its entropy, so the variance we want is the entropy of the answer, taken over meanings a:The same idea appears in the literature as semantic entropy (Kuhn, Gal and Farquhar, 2023).

H = −Σa p(a) log p(a)

One meaning with all the probability gives zero: no doubt. Probability shared across several meanings gives more, and the more evenly it is shared, the more doubt.

A naive first attempt

Meanings are expensive to enumerate. Tokens are not. At every step the model already holds a distribution over its next token, and the entropy of that distribution comes for free. So the first thing we tried was the obvious one: read doubt off the entropy of the token prediction.

The hope: if the model is unsure of an answer, the token where the answer begins should be a hard choice, and its entropy should show it. We tested that in two steps.

What token entropy showed

Answer tokens do carry the entropy. Take a typical reply from Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct: The director of the 2014 film "Pride" is Lee Chang-dong. Every token of the wording sits below 1 nat. The two tokens of the name sit at 5.1 and 4.0. The model is sure how to say it, and unsure what to say.

But high entropy does not mark an answer. In a long reasoning trace from DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, working on a programming puzzle, tokens nearly as uncertain turn up where no answer is at stake. After Wait, maybe I can, the model splits between have (14%), construct (9%) and try (9%): 3.3 nats, close to the uncertain name. The options are synonymous possibilities, many tokens with one meaning. The entropy is real; the doubt is not.

Token entropy rises where there is doubt, and also wherever there is more than one way to say something.

To separate the two, the spread has to be measured in meaning, not in tokens: the entropy of the answer, as defined above.
